At 9:45 PM on a Tuesday in September, twenty-five men from Midway stepped into the unsealed dust of the Lindon Utah Temple. We wore white jumpsuits over our clothes. Outside, the autumn air was cool against the foothills of Utah Valley, but inside, the building held a heavy, breathless silence. The drywall was unpainted in places, the floors were bare concrete, and temporary work lights cast long, stark shadows across the hallways.

We had not come to admire the architecture or to tour the finished rooms. We had come with rags and buckets to wipe construction dust from unsealed wood frames and brass hardware before the carpet layers arrived the next morning. There was no fanfare, no public notice, and no cameras. Just men who had finished their regular workdays, driving down the canyon to spend the late hours scrubbing what was not yet consecrated, but was already holy.

The assignment was specific. We were not allowed to touch the delicate glass panels or the altar surfaces. Our task was the periphery. We wiped baseboards, polished brass hinges, and dusted the intricate woodwork surrounding doorways that would soon open to receive thousands of families.

As the hours ticked past eleven o'clock, the fatigue in my arms began to set in. The repetitive motion of wiping dust from mahogany and oak became a rhythm of quiet reflection. I found myself alone for a few moments on the lower level, away from the low murmur of conversation and the scrape of buckets.

I walked toward the foot of the uncarpeted stairs leading up into the ordinance rooms. The stairs rose into the dim light above, flanked by bare walls and exposed studs. 

I paused.

In that stillness, away from the work and the noise, a distinct impression pressed upon my mind. It came with a clarity that silenced every thought of fatigue. The words were as distinct as if spoken aloud in the empty corridor: 

"You are worthy, my son."

The message did not arrive as an evaluation of my proficiency with a cleaning rag or a checklist of mortal accomplishments. It came as a quiet reminder of what priesthood service is designed to anchor in the human soul. Priesthood power is not found in titles or administrative prominence. It is found in the quiet willingness to tend the house of God in the dark hours, asking for nothing in return.

For two hours, those twenty-five men worked without speaking much. In the physical labor of cleaning dust from an unfinished temple, I understood something deeper about the cleansing required in our own lives. We often wait until our spiritual house is fully polished and finished before we feel worthy to draw near to the Savior. But the Lord meets us in the dust and the scaffolding. He meets us in the white jumpsuits of our ordinary, tired efforts when our hearts are turned toward His holy purposes.

The psalmist asked long ago: "Who shall ascend into the hill of the Lord? or who shall stand in his holy place? He that hath clean hands, and a pure heart; who hath not lifted up his soul unto vanity, nor sworn deceitfully." 

Standing at the foot of those stairs, I realized that clean hands are not achieved by our own righteousness alone, but by submitting our labor to His grace. The temple is not just a building of stone and wood; it is a physical manifestation of the order of heaven established among men. When we assist in preparing His house, our own spirits are ordered and refined.

As recorded in the Doctrine and Covenants regarding the dedication of the house of the Lord, His glory rests upon those who establish a house of prayer, a house of order, a house of God. That midnight shift in Lindon was a reminder that every act of humble service performed in His name draws the veil a little closer.

Art has long attempted to capture this sacred closeness. When midnight approached and our buckets were emptied, we changed out of our white jumpsuits and stepped back out into the cool Utah night. The contrast between the dust inside and the starlight above was sharp. My hands were dry and my shoulders ached, but my mind was completely at peace.

We do not always need grand platforms or public validation to feel the approval of heaven. Sometimes all it takes is a bucket, a rag, a quiet staircase, and the willingness to serve in the shadows.
