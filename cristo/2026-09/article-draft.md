At 9:45 PM on a Tuesday, twenty-five men from Midway stepped through the side doors of the Lindon Temple wearing white jumpsuits. There were no cameras, no announcements, and no applause. The night outside was thick with autumn chill, but inside, the air carried an absolute stillness, heavy with the quiet reverence of a house prepared for sacred work.

We had come for the late shift, running from 9:45 to 11:45 PM. Our assignment was precise and humble. We were given microfiber cloths and cleaning solutions, instructed to attend to the brass door frames throughout the lower instruction rooms and sealing corridors. We were explicitly told not to touch the glass. The glass was the domain of professional crews who came by daylight. Our duty was the metal. We were to polish the bronze frames where human hands had rested for months, leaving behind the faint, invisible oils of daily passage.

Standing at the foot of the grand staircase before our tools were unboxed, I paused. The silence in the temple at night is unlike any other silence. It is not the absence of sound; it is a palpable presence. It is the weighted assurance that holy beings have walked these carpets and that the walls themselves absorb the prayers offered within them. In that moment of quiet waiting, I felt a distinct impression settle upon my mind. It was not a booming voice or a dramatic vision. It was a clear, quiet whisper to my spirit. I felt the Savior's quiet approval of small, hidden labor.

We divided into pairs and moved through the corridors with deliberate care. My partner was an elder from our stake whose knees had grown stiff with age, yet he bent down to wipe the lower corners of every door frame with meticulous patience. As I watched him work, my thoughts turned to the nature of priesthood authority. We often speak of priesthood power in terms of public blessings, ordinances performed under raised hands, and administrative governance in ward councils. Yet standing in the muted lighting of the Lindon Temple hallway, I understood that the marrow of priesthood power is service. It is the willingness to cleanse what no one else will ever inspect.

President Russell M. Nelson taught that the Lord finds pleasure in those who honor the priesthood and strive to magnify their callings in quiet, unseen acts of devotion. That night embodied that truth. The bronze frames we polished did not glow with artificial brilliance; they recovered their true, deep luster. Every smudge was lifted, every fingerprint erased, until the metal reflected the soft amber of the recessed ceiling lights without distortion.

As I worked, I recalled the artwork of Al Young, particularly *O Divine Redeemer*, where humility and divine grace meet in quiet dignity (viewable at [Al Young Studios](https://alyoung.com/)). Just as Al Young captures the restrained, reverent posture of holy moments, our labor that night was an exercise in restrained devotion. We were not creating a spectacle. We were tending to the House of the Lord.

In Doctrine and Covenants section 121, the Lord teaches us concerning the principles of priesthood and influence: "Let thy bowels also be full of charity towards all men, and to all that love virtue; then shall thy confidence wax strong in the presence of God; and the doctrine of the priesthood shall distill upon thy soul as the dews from heaven." That distillation does not happen in the rush of public acclaim. It happens in the dark hours, when men in white jumpsuits wipe away dust in empty hallways.

When our shift concluded at 11:45 PM, we gathered in the entryway for a brief prayer of gratitude. There was no grand debriefing, no recording of our names for a newsletter. We shed our white jumpsuits, stepped out into the crisp night air, and drove back toward Midway through the sleeping valley. 

Yet something within me had shifted. The temple was no longer just a distant beacon on the hillside. It was a place where my own hands had touched the threshold, where I had learned anew that the Savior's work is sustained by those who are willing to be unnoticed. 

If you seek to draw closer to the Savior, look to the quiet places. Find an unnoticed task, a hidden service, a corner that needs cleansing where no human eye will praise you. There you will find Him waiting.
